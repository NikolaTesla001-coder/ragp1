The demon core was a 6.2-kilogram (14 lb) sphere of plutonium–gallium alloy with a diameter of 89 millimeters (3.5 inches) that was manufactured in 1945 by the Manhattan Project as the fissile core for a potential third atomic bomb.  Originally intended for deployment against Japan, the core’s fate changed after the war, leading scientists at Los Alamos Laboratory to use it for experimental "tickling the dragon's tail" tests to determine the precise point of criticality.  These experiments involved surrounding the subcritical mass with neutron reflectors—specifically tungsten carbide bricks or beryllium hemispheres—to bounce escaping neutrons back into the core, thereby increasing the likelihood of a sustained nuclear chain reaction. 
The core earned its sinister nickname after causing two fatal criticality accidents in 1945 and 1946, both of which resulted in the acute radiation poisoning and subsequent death of the scientists involved.  In the first incident on August 21, 1945, physicist Harry Daghlian accidentally dropped a tungsten carbide brick onto the assembly, pushing it into a supercritical state and releasing a lethal burst of neutron and gamma radiation that killed him 25 days later.  The second, more notorious accident occurred on May 21, 1946, when physicist Louis Slotin was manually holding apart the two halves of a beryllium tamper using only a flathead screwdriver; the screwdriver slipped, allowing the reflector to fully enclose the core and cause an instantaneous runaway chain reaction that released a visible flash of blue light and a deadly radiation pulse, killing Slotin nine days later. 
Following the second tragedy, the plutonium sphere was deemed too dangerous for further handling or testing, effectively ending its role in nuclear weapon development. Plans to use it in Operation Crossroads, a series of post-war nuclear weapon tests at Bikini Atoll, were canceled due to the core's heightened radiation levels and the excessive contamination observed in previous tests.  Consequently, the demon core was melted down in the summer of 1946, and its plutonium-239 was recycled and recast into new cores for the United States’ Cold War nuclear stockpile, ensuring that the material persisted even though the original object no longer exists.
